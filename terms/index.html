5. Accuracy and limits of the model

5.1 Results are estimates from a simplified model. Sketch CFD calculates with a simplified two-dimensional model, the lattice Boltzmann method on a square grid. A planar model is a slice of a set depth and assumes that nothing changes across that depth. An axisymmetric model assumes a round part with no swirl. Real flows are three-dimensional, so results can differ from real behavior by a large amount.

5.2 What the model leaves out or simplifies.

  • Three-dimensional flow.
  • Compressible and supersonic flow, combustion and chemical reactions, free surfaces such as a water level, and multiphase flow.
  • Fluid properties that change with temperature. Viscosity and diffusivity stay fixed, and a heated gas does not expand. Warm fluid and light or heavy gases act only through a buoyancy force.
  • Turbulence. It is represented by one simple eddy-viscosity model, and no turbulent benchmark case has been checked. Turbulent jets, rooms and wakes are therefore rough estimates. Two-dimensional jets spread several times faster than real jets.
  • Heavy gases. A heavy gas such as carbon dioxide spreading along a floor may arrive earlier than it would in reality.
  • Hot gases. At high temperatures the real viscosity and diffusivity of a gas are much larger than the fixed values the model uses.
  • Coarse grids. On a coarse grid the model raises the viscosity to keep the calculation stable, so the flow behaves as if it were slower or more viscous than the values entered.
  • Speed. Results hold only at moderate flow speeds on the chosen grid; the app warns when a run goes beyond them.
  • Open edges. They take gas in as if from still air.
  • Particles. They do not affect the flow, have no inertia and do not collide, and each run uses one particle size. Particle results are random and differ from run to run.
  • Porous parts. Capture of particles and tracer by a filter follows the value you set; it is not calculated from a theory of capture.
  • The paper import. It can miss or misread parts, and it does not read handwriting. Check every part, role and value on the review screen before you use an imported sketch.

Help and shortcuts > Model in the app describes the model in more detail.

5.3 Verified against benchmark cases, not validated against the real world. The calculation has been verified against published benchmark cases and exact solutions. Its results were compared with published values, and each comparison had to fall within a set tolerance: 1% for exact solutions and mass or heat balances, 2% for steady benchmarks, 3% for unsteady peaks and recirculation lengths, and 5% for gravity currents. The cases are:

  • flow in a lid-driven square cavity at Reynolds numbers 100 and 1000: Ghia, U., Ghia, K. N. and Shin, C. T. (1982), Journal of Computational Physics 48, 387–411;
  • laminar flow around a cylinder in a channel, benchmark cases 2D-1 and 2D-2: Schäfer, M. and Turek, S. (1996), in Flow Simulation with High-Performance Computers II, Vieweg, 547–566;
  • flow over a backward-facing step at Reynolds number 389, reattachment length: Rose, A. and Simpson, B. (2000), First NAFEMS Workbook of CFD Examples, and Gresho, P. M. and Sani, R. L. (2000), Incompressible Flow and the Finite Element Method; geometry after Armaly, B. F. et al. (1983), Journal of Fluid Mechanics 127, 473–496;
  • natural convection in a heated square cavity at Rayleigh numbers 10³ to 10⁵: de Vahl Davis, G. (1983), International Journal for Numerical Methods in Fluids 3, 249–264;
  • the front speed of a gravity current: Härtel, C., Meiburg, E. and Necker, F. (2000), Journal of Fluid Mechanics 418, 189–212;
  • exact solutions and balances for flow in channels, pipes and between moving walls, and for heat conduction: Bird, R. B., Stewart, W. E. and Lightfoot, E. N. (2002), Transport Phenomena; White, F. M. (2006), Viscous Fluid Flow; Shah, R. K. and London, A. L. (1978), Laminar Flow Forced Convection in Ducts;
  • diffusion: Crank, J. (1975), The Mathematics of Diffusion;
  • flow through porous layers (Darcy and Forchheimer laws);
  • particle settling, Brownian motion and thermophoresis: Hinds, W. C. (1999), Aerosol Technology, and Talbot, L. et al. (1980), Journal of Fluid Mechanics 101, 737–758.

These checks show that the model’s equations are solved as intended for those cases. They do not show that the model represents any real room, building, product or installation. Sketch CFD has not been validated against measurements, physical tests or certified engineering software, and it has not been tested in real-world scenarios. The checks were run on a desktop computer with the same calculation code; results on an iPhone or iPad can differ slightly.
